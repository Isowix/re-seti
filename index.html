Why now

The archives aged. The methods did too.

The original SETI@home project distributed work from 1999 until it stopped sending tasks in 2020. It searched for a narrow kind of signal with the tools of that era, and it did not confirm a technosignature. That null result is the starting point, not an embarrassment. A null is only as strong as the search that produced it.

01

Pattern recognition moved

Variational autoencoders, density-based clustering, and modern anomaly detection are built for rare structure in messy data. They were not the tools inside the original SETI@home client. Asking whether they would surface anything that client missed is a reasonable scientific question. It is not a claim that the earlier work was careless.

02

Others have already started

This proposal does not claim to be the first use of machine learning in radio SETI. A 2026 review of Breakthrough Listen’s first decade describes unsupervised anomaly-detection campaigns on Green Bank data, and injection-and-recovery tests as part of how those searches are checked. The open question is narrower: whether an independent, documented reprocessing — with volunteer-scale compute, public methods, and a published null if that is the outcome — still adds something.

03

A null can be the product

If a careful rerun finds nothing that survives review, that result is worth writing down. It becomes a benchmark, and training material, for whoever analyzes the next telescope’s data. The point of the project is the documented outcome, not a discovery announcement.

The premise

A reanalysis. Not a new sky survey.

Given what was already recorded, would today’s pattern recognition surface candidates that earlier pipelines missed? The proposal is scoped to that question. It is in review. No organization has been formed, no funding has been raised, and no computational infrastructure has been stood up. Further steps wait on human review, and on at least one other person willing to take the work further.

What it would try to do

  • Reprocess publicly available, or previously analyzed, radio SETI archives — including Breakthrough Listen public data and the original SETI@home corpus where those data are actually accessible.
  • Use unsupervised and semi-supervised methods suited to rare events: variational autoencoders, density-based clustering, and anomaly detection.
  • Where a large model is useful, run it in a memory-efficient way, a layer at a time if needed, so a consumer GPU could take part through a BOINC-style client.
  • Keep a small coordination service — the “Master Node” in the notes — for handing out work, collecting results, and tracking which checks a candidate has passed.
  • Treat a negative result as a result, and publish the method either way.

What this is not

  • Not a request for immediate funding, incorporation, or a co-founder.
  • Not an assertion that earlier SETI work was flawed.
  • Not a plan to operate telescopes or to collect new data in the near term.
  • Not tied to any single model, library, or vendor.
  • Not a claim that extraterrestrial intelligence will be found.
  • Not a partnership with any observatory, university, funding agency, or existing SETI program. No such body endorses this page.

Methodology

Six steps, from spectrogram to a candidate worth a person’s time

The pipeline below is the scientific shape of the proposal. It is not software that runs today. AI is allowed to nominate. It is not allowed to conclude. The picture in the header is the kind of data step one would start from.

  1. Raw spectrograms

    Start from time–frequency spectrograms: intensity across frequency and time, the usual picture of a radio observation. The input is archival data from radio telescopes, not a new campaign. Which files are public, complete, and worth the compute is one of the questions for reviewers.

  2. Preprocessing

    Clean, calibrate, and put observations on a common scale before any model sees them. The usual enemies are instrumental drift and radio-frequency interference — terrestrial signals that can look interesting if you are careless.

    • Flag radio-frequency interference.
    • Calibrate.
    • Normalize.

  3. AI anomaly detection

    Two views of one idea: learn what ordinary data looks like, then flag what does not fit. High reconstruction error, or a point outside a dense cluster, is a reason to look. It is not a discovery.

    Variational autoencoder

    A spectrogram is compressed into a smaller latent description and then rebuilt. The model is trained on the bulk of “normal” structure. A patch that will not rebuild cleanly is the anomaly. No such model has been trained for this proposal.

    Density clustering

    Similar signals are grouped. Points that fall outside the dense groups are kept for ranking. The notes name HDBSCAN as a concrete algorithm. The requirement is density-based clustering, not one library.

  4. Ranking

    Anomalies would be ordered by how unusual they are, how rarely that pattern appears, and how strong they are against the local noise. A rank decides what a person reads next. It does not decide what is true. No scores exist, because no data has been scored.

  5. Injected-signal validation

    Known synthetic signals are buried in real observations. The pipeline earns trust only if it recovers those signals at a measured rate, and if it does not light up in the same way where nothing was planted. This test is mandatory in the proposal. It has not been built.

  6. Multi-telescope confirmation

    A hit in one archive does not establish an astrophysical origin. The proposal requires a second instrument, and a human review, before any candidate is treated as interesting. No observatory has been asked, and none has agreed.

Architecture

A Master Node, public archives, and GPUs that have not joined

Read this as a proposal diagram. The Master Node is not running. No archive is connected. No volunteer computer has joined, and there is no client to install.

Public archives in

The design assumes raw observations, filterbank products, and metadata from programs that already release data, Breakthrough Listen among them. Telescopes named on the diagram — Green Bank, Parkes, MeerKAT, and others — are examples of where such data has come from. Naming them is not a data-sharing agreement.

Master Node

A small, fault-tolerant coordination service on cloud virtual machines, with failover. It would schedule work, collect returned results, and keep the validation state of each candidate. “Resilient” here means the design wants a spare. It does not mean a cluster exists.

Volunteer GPU nodes

The client in the notes is BOINC plus Docker, running memory-efficient, layer-wise inference so a larger model can pass through a consumer GPU a layer at a time. AirLLM is one open technique of that kind, not a commitment to a product. The tagline “Your GPU” is the ambition. The count of volunteers today is zero.

Phased plan

Five phases, none of them started

The months below are counted from a future start that has not been set. They describe sequence, so a reviewer can see what would have to come first. They are not a schedule, a budget, or a staff plan.

  1. Phase 1 · Months 1–3

    Foundation

    Find out which data can actually be used, and on what terms. Stand up a small Master Node on cloud virtual machines with failover. Prototype a client that can run a memory-efficient model inside Docker, under BOINC.

  2. Phase 2 · Months 3–6

    AI pipeline

    Implement a variational autoencoder and density-based clustering. Build the injected-signal suite before any ranking is trusted. The test comes before the leaderboard.

  3. Phase 3 · Months 6–9

    Pilot

    A volunteer beta, with Dockerized clients, on a modest public subset. The pilot exists to measure false positives and the recovery of injected signals. It does not exist to announce candidates.

  4. Phase 4 · Months 9–18

    Scale

    Only if the pilot’s validation holds: extend processing toward the archive that reviewers and data holders have actually agreed to, with continuous monitoring and ranking.

  5. Phase 5 · Ongoing

    Follow-up

    Anything that still ranks high goes to a person. Only after that would anyone ask an observatory for independent observing time. Nothing of that kind is arranged. Results, including a null, would be written up in public.

Trust but verify

A candidate is boring until it survives

Every stage is meant to be reproducible and auditable. The proposal rejects unsubstantiated claims. Sensational findings are a failure mode, not a goal. No one should describe a hit from this effort as interesting until the checks below have happened — and those checks are a policy, not a pipeline that exists.

Injected signals

Synthetic technosignatures, planted into real data by someone who knows the answer, are the calibration. Recovery rate, and the rate of false excitement on unplanted data, are the numbers that would matter.

A second telescope

A signal that appears in one recording and nowhere else is, until proven otherwise, interference or a processing artifact. Confirmation means an independent instrument, not a second pass of the same file.

A person, before the adjective

Human review sits in front of any decision to treat a candidate as interesting, to ask for follow-up time, or to say so in public. Automation can sort. It cannot declare.

The null, on the record

If the checks wipe the slate clean, that is the result to publish. Methods, models, and negative findings would be written so someone else can rerun them. Open science here is a constraint, not a slogan.

Funding and partnerships

Avenues, not commitments

No money has been raised. No grant has been filed. No sponsor, foundation, agency, observatory, or company is attached to this proposal. The four paths below are ways a project like this could someday be supported. They are not a forecast.

Private philanthropy

People and foundations that already fund basic science are the obvious place a later request could go. The 2015 Breakthrough Listen commitment, linked above, is a historical example of philanthropic SETI funding. It belongs to that program.

Public-science grants

Citizen-science and research grants are a possible later application. None has been written. A long history of public funding for astronomy outreach is not support for SETI@Home 2, and no funding agency is a partner here.

Data access, not a telescope of our own

The work needs permission to read data, or a clear public license that already grants it. An observatory named in a diagram is a place data has historically been taken. It is not a collaborator. No agreement has been asked for or signed.

In-kind and open-source help

Sponsored cloud credits, community tooling, or advice from people who run volunteer-computing projects could matter later, because the operating idea is deliberately lean. Nobody has offered that help. There is no BOINC project to join.

Call for reviewers

Read it, then try to break it

Phil Rail is asking for a small number of people to sanity-check the idea before any further commitment. Skeptical comments are as useful as encouraging ones. You are not being asked for money, time on a telescope, or to co-found anything. A short note is enough. There is no deadline.

  1. Is the scientific premise sound — is a modern reanalysis of existing radio SETI archives a reasonable next step?
  2. Are injected signals, a second telescope, and human review enough to keep the effort credible?
  3. Which technical, statistical, or social risks does the outline under-weight?
  4. Which parts of the public archives are realistically accessible, and worth doing first?
  5. Does the framing stay clear without over-claiming, and is it clear enough to attract competent collaborators?
