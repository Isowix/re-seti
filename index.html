RE-SETI

Would today's methods find what earlier searches missed?
Trust but verify.

A proposal to re-read archival radio SETI data and to publish a measured result either way.

Stage: proposal in review.

Full proposal archive (zip)

Why look again?

SETI@home ran as a volunteer computing project from 1999 until it stopped distributing work on 31 March 2020, without a confirmed technosignature. A null result is only as strong as the search that produced it. Asking whether newer methods would surface anything the original client missed is a reasonable question, not a claim that the earlier work was careless.

Sources: Anderson et al., Communications of the ACM 45, 56–61 (2002), doi:10.1145/581571.581573; SETI@home project news, 2 March 2020; Anderson et al. and Korpela et al., The Astronomical Journal 170, 111 and 112 (2025).

Variational autoencoders, density-based clustering, and modern anomaly detection are built for rare structure in messy data. They were not the tools inside the original SETI@home client.

This proposal does not claim to be the first use of machine learning in radio SETI. A 2026 review of Breakthrough Listen’s first decade describes unsupervised anomaly-detection campaigns on Green Bank data, among them the GLOBULAR clustering method of Jacobson-Bell et al. (2025), which applies HDBSCAN to turboSETI hits, and injection-and-recovery tests as part of how those searches are checked. GLOBULAR’s published trade-off is far fewer false positives, with some injected signals lost. Sources: Jacobson-Bell et al., The Astronomical Journal 169, 206 (2025), doi:10.3847/1538-3881/adb8e7; MacMahon and Czech, arXiv:2610.07482 (2026).

On 20 July 2015, Breakthrough Listen was announced as a ten-year program with a stated commitment of $100 million, and with the intention that the data be open. That commitment funded Breakthrough Listen, not RE-SETI (Breakthrough Initiatives, announcement). Data Release 1.0 was a defined public set of almost 1 petabyte of raw and reduced data (Lebofsky et al., 2019, Publications of the Astronomical Society of the Pacific). As of March 2026, Breakthrough Listen storage across telescope sites and the archive was about 42 PB of capacity, with about 31 PB in use — an inventory of their systems, not a single public download, and this proposal has no access agreement (the same MacMahon and Czech paper).

Nothing in this section is a claim that this proposal holds the data or the money.

A useful product can be a documented null: completeness measured on every reanalyzed subset, turboSETI, a β-VAE and GLOBULAR compared on identical data, an open pipeline, and a labelled benchmark for future telescopes.

What would be tested first?

The first, falsifiable test is whether an anomaly-detection stage can recover narrowband signals whose frequency drift is not constant. turboSETI’s search uses a grid of constant drift rates and is not designed for that class. Ma et al. (2023) reported eight signals of interest from a cadence-aware β-VAE search, and explicitly set aside signals whose drift changed between ON scans. Re-observations on 21 May 2022 did not re-detect those eight; the paper reported they are not persistent and did not claim a detection. They are not treated here as open candidates. A re-reduction of the public data can still be a methods check. Published injection tests have mostly used linear drift, so the evidence that a new method recovers non-linear drift is thin. The pilot is built to measure that claim, with go/kill criteria; it is not a premise.

How would it work?

The pipeline is a design, not software that runs today. A model may nominate; it may not conclude. The design does not depend on any single model, library, or vendor.

Test signals are planted in the raw or lightly prepared data, then flagging, detection and ranking are run, so the recovery rate measures the whole chain:

  1. Spectrogram — time against frequency, from an archive, not a new campaign.
  2. Inject — plant the test signals.
  3. Clean — flag interference, calibrate, normalize.
  4. Detect — a model nominates what does not fit.
  5. Rank — order what is unusual, rare, and strong against the local noise. A rank decides what a person reads next, not what is true.
  6. Score — apply a pre-registered threshold.

A variational autoencoder would be trained on the bulk of “normal” structure. “Normal” means the empirical archive, including ordinary interference, not a clean sky. Training follows Ma et al. (2023): it is cadence-aware (ON versus OFF), uses real backgrounds with synthetic labels, and holds out known dirty bands. Residual contamination of backgrounds is a known risk, not a solved one. A patch that will not rebuild cleanly is the anomaly. No such model has been trained for this proposal.

Density clustering would group similar signals and keep points outside the dense groups for ranking. The notes would run GLOBULAR and also test clustering on the learned representations, and would re-measure GLOBULAR’s published trade-off on this set.

No scores exist, because no data has been scored. Score thresholds and a false-discovery-rate rule (for example Benjamini–Hochberg, with the empirical null taken from empty and shuffled-cadence controls) would be registered before any large-archive run.

Where would it start?

In parallel with review, on one consumer GPU: reproduce the published turboSETI event toward HIP 100064 in Price et al. (2020), Fig. 7c, doi:10.3847/1538-3881/ab65f1, from the public Data Release 1 cadence. Price later rejected the event as interference; the test is to recover the published detection, not to reopen it. Then run end-to-end injections of constant-drift and non-linear-drift signals, with go/kill criteria for the methods claim and, separately, for any volunteer network.

If the pilot holds

Months are estimates, counted from the end of Phase 0.

Phase 1 · Months 1–3. Learn what public data can actually be read, and on what terms, including Breakthrough Listen public data and the original SETI@home corpus where those data are actually accessible. Ask data holders about reducing data near the archive and agree a transfer budget. Stand up a small Master Node.

Phase 2 · Months 3–6. Train the autoencoder and the clustering stage on a pilot set.

Phase 3 · Months 6–9. Scale out the way the Phase 0 numbers chose: a small volunteer beta in containers, or rented GPUs. Compare against a single reference run. Publish the pilot result and the measured transfer and compute costs.

Phase 4 · Months 9–18. Only if the pilot holds: process the agreed archive in priority order, with a sensitivity measurement on every subset.

Phase 5 · Ongoing. Publish outcomes, including ruled-out candidates.

How would it be built?

This is a proposal diagram. The Master Node is not running, no archive is connected, no volunteer computer has joined, and there is no client to install.

Public archives would supply observations and filterbank products already on disk, only where the terms allow a reread, plus metadata — which telescope, which time, which band — without which a hit cannot be checked. Telescopes named on the diagram — Green Bank, Parkes, MeerKAT, and others — are examples of where such data has come from, not partners and not a data-sharing agreement.

Moving data, not computing, is expected to be the main cost, so the design filters and reduces data at or near the archive and moves only reduced, prioritized subsets. The arithmetic is in the architecture notes.

The Master Node would be a small coordination service on cloud virtual machines, with a spare for failover. It would schedule work, collect returned results, and keep the validation state of each candidate. “Resilient” here means the design wants a spare, not that a cluster exists.

Volunteer GPUs would be used only if the Phase 0 numbers justify them, running small detection models that fit on a consumer GPU. The client in the notes is BOINC plus Docker. It does not use AirLLM or layer-by-layer loading. Each work unit would go to at least two machines with different hardware, results would be compared within tolerances, and planted canary signals would catch faulty clients.

Model weights would be sent to the nodes, with room for feedback. A result record would keep what was processed, what failed, and the provenance of both.

How would later work be supported?

No funding has been sought or raised, and no partnership exists. These are later possibilities, not a forecast, and none of them has been opened.

Private philanthropy: people and foundations that already fund basic science.

Public-science grants: a possible later application; none has been written. A long history of public funding for astronomy outreach is not support for RE-SETI, and no funding agency is a partner here.

Data access: the work needs permission to read data, or a clear public license that already grants it. No agreement has been asked for or signed.

In-kind help: sponsored cloud credits, community tooling, or advice from people who run volunteer-computing projects could matter later. Nobody has offered that help, and there is no BOINC project to join.

Precedents live in the funding note, not a budget for RE-SETI.

How would a result be checked?

No candidate from this effort should be called interesting until the checks below have happened. Those checks are a policy, not a pipeline that exists. Sensational findings are a failure mode, not a goal.

No search has been run. The funnel is a metaphor for how strict the later gates are; the curves are the required shape of the injection test.

  1. Raw hitsEverything bright in the spectrogram, including interference.
  2. After RFI flagsThe lines that sit still, and the flashes that hit every channel, come out.
  3. Model anomaliesWhat is unusual, not merely loud. A nomination, not a finding.
  4. Passes the injection testOnly if planted signals return, and empty stretches stay quiet.
  5. A person reads itBefore anyone treats it as interesting.

The upper curve should rise as planted tones get stronger; the lower curve, where nothing was planted, should stay low. If both climb, ranking is not ready. No recovery rate exists yet.

The first check is an archival cross-match: the same sky position in other public datasets or epochs. New observations on a second telescope are out of scope until a partner exists. A person still reviews any high-ranking candidate, and sits in front of any decision to treat it as interesting, to ask for follow-up time, or to say so in public.

Methods, models, and negative findings would be written so someone else can rerun them.

What is being asked?

Phil Rail is asking for a small number of people to sanity-check the idea before any further commitment. Skeptical comments are as useful as encouraging ones. Scientific or technical collaboration is welcome. This is not a request for money, time on a telescope, funding, incorporation, or a business co-founder. A short note is enough. There is no deadline. Further work waits on that review, and on at least one other person willing to take it further. The proposal does not claim that extraterrestrial intelligence will be found.

  1. Is the scientific premise sound — is a modern reanalysis of existing radio SETI archives a reasonable next step?
  2. Are end-to-end injections, an archival cross-match, and human review enough to keep the effort credible?
  3. Which technical, statistical, or social risks does the outline under-weight?
  4. Which parts of the public archives are realistically accessible, and worth doing first?
  5. Does the framing stay clear without over-claiming, and is it clear enough to attract competent collaborators?
